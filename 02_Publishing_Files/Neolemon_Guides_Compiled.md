A real story has a cast: a main character, a sidekick, a parent, a place they all share. This guide shows you how to illustrate a children's book with AI when multiple characters and one world have to stay consistent across every page, from written story to a shareable flipbook. It's the companion to our free masterclass, and every prompt we used is included below.The short answer: how to illustrate a children's book with AIBreak your story into three lists: characters, backgrounds, and what each page shows. Generate each character once in Character Turbo as a full-body, front-view image on a plain white background, and each recurring location once in Background Generator. When a scene needs something different, use the editors to create variations instead of regenerating from scratch. Then compose each page in Story Scene Pro by tagging your references (@girl, @farmhouse) in the scene description, and use Editor Pro for small changes within the same scene. Finally, assemble the pages in Projects, add covers and text in Canvas, and share the finished book as a flipbook.Step 1: Break down your storyEverything starts here, and it's where most people go wrong. Pasting your whole story into an AI tool and expecting a full set of illustrations back doesn't work. Before generating a single image, pull three lists out of the story: who is in it (characters), where it happens (backgrounds), and what each page needs to show (scenes). Get this right and the rest of the workflow almost runs itself.The masterclass works with this short story:Ellie loved summer mornings at the farmhouse. She wandered into the meadow to gather a bunch of wildflowers, one for every color she could find. As she knelt in the tall grass, something caught her eye. Little paw prints, pressed fresh into the soft dirt path. Ellie set her flowers down and looked closer. The tracks were small and round, and they led off toward the farmhouse. Whose paws could they be? She picked up her bouquet and followed along, wondering if someone had wandered off all alone. Then the grass rustled, and a little golden puppy came bounding out, straight toward her. He wore a shiny blue collar and wagged his whole body with joy. As Ellie knelt to say hello, the farmhouse door swung open, and out stepped Dad with a knowing smile. "I wondered when you two would find each other," Dad said from the porch. "He's yours to look after now." Ellie held out her hand, and the puppy placed one soft paw in hers, as if to say hello. Ellie named him Biscuit. And from that morning on, every day at the farmhouse began the very same way, with wildflowers, wagging tails, and belly rubs in the warm summer sun.Broken down, the story needs three characters, one background, and eight scenes. Write a detailed visual description for each character and background. These become your generation prompts in Step 2.Characters@girl - A young girl, around six years old. Long wavy golden-blonde hair with a small flower tucked behind one ear, big brown eyes, green ruffled long-sleeve top, blue denim overalls rolled at the ankle, brown lace-up boots.@dog - A fluffy light-golden retriever puppy with floppy ears, a bright blue collar, and a round paw-print tag.@father - A round friendly farmer with a bushy mustache, straw sun hat, blue-and-white checked shirt, and denim overalls.Backgrounds@farmhouse - a rustic wooden farmhouse with a covered porch, red-brown roof, and stone chimney, a small barn beside it, a golden meadow full of red, white, and purple wildflowers with a winding dirt path in front, rolling green hills and trees behind, blue sky with soft clouds.ScenesEach scene is a character (or several) plus a background plus the moment the page needs to show:Page 1: Ellie in the meadow, holding wildflowers, happy.Page 2: Ellie kneeling, discovering paw prints on the path.Pages 3 and 4: Ellie standing, looking around, curious and puzzled about whose tracks they are.Page 5: the puppy bounding out of the grass toward Ellie.Page 6: Ellie greeting the puppy as Dad appears at the farmhouse door.Page 7: the paw-shake between Ellie and the puppy, Dad watching from the porch.Page 8: Ellie rubbing Biscuit's belly, Dad smiling from the porch.Covers. Front: Ellie and Biscuit running through the meadow with the title. Back: the empty path with paw prints leading to the farmhouse.You don't need to illustrate everything in the story. Pick the specific shots and moments that carry it.Step 2: Generate your characters and backgroundsGenerate each character and background once, then reuse them in every scene. That single decision is what keeps a character looking identical from page to page.The rule that makes everything else work: every character is a full-body, front-view illustration on a plain white background. No story scenes yet, no actions or poses. When the AI has a clean, complete reference, it can create new poses and actions while keeping consistency and proportions. These anchor images become the reference for everything that follows.Here's how simple a character description can be. This one, from the masterclass, was created in Character Turbo:a joyful 5-year-old boy with brown eyes and curly brown hair. he wears a green t-shirt, denim shorts, white sneakers and orange round glasses.Set the action and expression to a full-body front view, the background to plain white, pick your aspect ratio, and choose a style. Pixar inspired is the most popular choice among our users. For the farmhouse story, the same approach produces the girl, the dog, and the father from the descriptions in Step 1, plus the farmhouse background. Here are all four assets from the masterclass:Recurring locations get the same treatment in Background Generator, so your scenes share one world instead of getting a new random setting each time. The masterclass example:a cozy and inviting room, bathed in soft, warm light filtering through a large window. a vibrant, cheerful orange sofa located in the center, adorned with an assortment of colorful pillows. lush, leafy green plants in terracotta pots are placed around the room. and a whimsical dreamcatcher hangs delicately near the window, catching the light.If your book has one main character and simple settings, our single-character workflow guide covers that lighter version of this process.Step 3: Create variations with the editorsIf a scene needs something different, use the editors to create a variation: a new action, expression, or outfit for a character, or different lighting for a background. In the masterclass, the girl gets a braid and a new outfit, and the farmhouse goes from a sunny afternoon to nighttime, which turns one background into a bedtime scene.Every image you generate carries the editor buttons, and they are designed to keep the consistency of your original character. The masterclass walks the boy character through a full variation chain:Editor Pro: reading a red book while standing with very focused expression. He is standing in a sunny summer park.Edit Expression: amazedEdit Action: jumping in the airEdit Background: a sunny beachOne rule matters more than the rest: when you want a completely different action or a brand-new scene, go back to the original full-body, white-background image and edit from there. In the reading image above, the book blocks most of the character, so a new action generated from it won't come out well. Use the grid icon to find your original image fast, or favorite it so it's always one click away.Step 4: Compose your scenesThis is where the book comes together. Two tools do the work:Story Scene Pro builds a fresh scene from tagged assets. Upload or select your character and background references, then type @ in the scene description to tag them.Editor Pro takes the previous scene and changes only what the prompt asks for, keeping everything else identical.Alternate between them: compose a new moment, then nudge it forward. Always tag your references in the scene description. A plain text prompt without tags will not work as effectively.Here is the complete scene sequence from the masterclass, every prompt verbatim:Scene 1 (Story Scene Pro): @girl walking through the flower field near @farmhouse. She is picking wildflowers with happy smile on a sunny dayScene 2 (Editor Pro): The girl walks further to the front and discovers the paw prints of a small puppy. With a curious expression, she kneels to investigate the paw prints. Keep everything else exactly the same as in original scene. Keep the same art style as original image.Scene 3 (Editor Pro): The girl is standing and looking around confusedScene 4 (Story Scene Pro, adds the dog): @girl is standing in front the @farmhouse and smiling with excitement @dog running happily toward @girl, tail waggingScene 5 (Story Scene Pro, adds the father): @girl is playing with the @dog in front of the @farmhouse, while the @father is standing behind them at the entrance of the farmhouse door which is now open. The @father has a surprised expression.Scene 6 (Editor Pro): The father at the farmhouse door is smiling warmly while the girl plays with the dog. The dog is lying on its back, happy, while the girl tickles the dog. Keep the same art style as original image.Scene 7 (bonus, Story Scene Pro): @cow walking over curiously, happy playing together in front of the farmhouse, sunny afternoon, @father smiling warmly while @girl plays with @dogA few workflow details worth knowing. You can add up to six characters in one scene, plus an object reference (something a character should hold) and a style reference (an illustration whose look you want to match). Everything you upload appears under recent uploads, so you never re-upload the same reference twice. And notice the pattern in the sequence above: new character enters, use Story Scene Pro; same cast, new moment, use Editor Pro.When all scenes are done, add the page text and speech bubbles. For precise control over layouts with several characters, see the multi-character Canvas workflow.Step 5: Assemble your bookCreate a project in Projects, and it asks for a front cover, a back cover, and pages.Build the covers in Canvas. Choose the KDP print size, the 8.5" x 8.5" square picture book, and Canvas shows margin and trim guidelines, so nothing important crosses the blue dotted line. Add your cover scene, set it as the background, then add a blob, your title text, and your author name. For scenes, the same Canvas handles speech bubbles: add the image, pick a bubble, type what the character says. Export each page as a PNG. The full tour is in AI Canvas: Build and Compose Your Story Pages.Back in the project, add your pages, then arrange them in Grid view and read the book through in Storybook view to catch anything out of order. Preview it as a Flip Book, and use Share to send it as a link to family and friends. From written story to finished picture book, in five steps.FAQWhy does my character look different in every scene?Almost always because a new scene was generated from a previous scene instead of the original reference. Go back to your full-body, front-view, white-background anchor image and generate from there. In Story Scene Pro, make sure every character reference is that clean anchor image, and always tag it in the scene description.How many characters can one scene have?Story Scene Pro supports up to six characters in a single scene, plus an object reference and a style reference. Name your tags (@girl reads better than @character1) and use the exact tag in the scene description.Do I need drawing skills or AI experience?No. The workflow is built for beginners: describe your characters in plain language, and the tools handle poses, expressions, and scenes from your references. Most Neolemon authors have never illustrated anything before.Can I print my book or sell it on Amazon KDP?Yes. Build your covers and pages at the KDP print size in Canvas and upscale your exports for print quality. PDF export of interior pages and covers directly from Projects is shipping soon. Note that KDP requires you to disclose AI-generated content at upload.Ready to illustrate your story?If your story has been sitting in a drawer because the illustrations felt impossible, this is the workflow that gets it out. Do not indexCanonical URLneolemon.com/guides/...eolemon/If you're planning to publish your children's book on Amazon KDP, IngramSpark, or any print service, your illustrations need to be high resolution. Upscaling images in Neolemon is completely free, takes a couple of clicks, and produces print-ready 300 DPI output for the most popular children's book sizes. Here's exactly how to do it.Why You Should Always Upscale Before DownloadingPrint-on-demand platforms like Amazon KDP reject files that don't meet their resolution requirements. Neolemon generates images at a base resolution of 1024 x 1024 pixels for 1:1 aspect ratio images. That looks fine on your screen, but it's only about 120 DPI at 8.5" print size, which means blurry output on paper.The built-in 2.5x upscale brings your image to 2560 x 2560 pixels, completely free and without consuming any credits. At that resolution, you hit ~301 DPI for the popular 8.5" x 8.5" KDP format, meeting the 300 DPI standard required for professional printing. No external tools needed, no extra cost, no workarounds.The rule of thumb: always upscale before you download. It's the last step before export, it costs you nothing, and skipping it is the most common mistake new creators make.How to Upscale an Image in NeolemonStep 1: Open your character chat. Navigate to the character or project where your illustration lives. Find the specific image you want to upscale.Step 2: Hover over the image. When you hover, a toolbar appears with several options: Upscale, Transparent Background, Download, Edit Action, and more.Step 3: Click "Upscale." Neolemon processes the image and scales it 2.5x, from 1024 x 1024 to 2560 x 2560 pixels. Wait a few seconds for it to finish. This is completely free and does not use any of your credits.Step 4: Download the upscaled version. Once processing is complete, click Download to save the high-resolution file to your device.That's it. Four steps, maybe 30 seconds per image, and zero credits spent.What Resolution Do You Actually Need?Here's how the 2.5x upscaled output (2560 x 2560) maps to common children's book sizes at print:6" x 6" book: 2560 pixels ÷ 6 inches = ~427 DPI. Well above the 300 DPI standard. Your illustrations will be razor sharp.8" x 8" book: 2560 pixels ÷ 8 inches = 320 DPI. Comfortably above the 300 DPI threshold.8.5" x 8.5" book (popular KDP size): 2560 pixels ÷ 8.5 inches = ~301 DPI. Right at the 300 DPI sweet spot. This is the format most of our creators publish in, and the 2.5x upscale was built specifically to cover it.The short version: for every standard children's book size up to 8.5" x 8.5", the free upscale meets or exceeds 300 DPI. You're covered.Tips for Upscaling Your Book IllustrationsUpscale every image in your book, not just the cover. Interior pages need to meet the same resolution standards. If you're working on a 24-page picture book, run through each illustration before assembling your final PDF. Since upscaling is free, there's no reason not to.Upscale after you're done editing. If you're still tweaking expressions, swapping outfits, or changing backgrounds, wait until you've locked in the final version. Finish your edits with the Expression Editor and Action Editor first, then upscale as your last step before download.Use the upscaled version for your cover too. Your cover is the first thing readers see, both as a thumbnail on Amazon and as a physical book in someone's hands. If you're designing it in Canva or another tool, start with the upscaled image so the final output is crisp at any size.For the full workflow from character creation to finished KDP book, check out How to Create Consistent AI Cartoon Characters, which walks through every step including the final export and upscaling process.FAQDoes upscaling cost credits in Neolemon?

Do not indexCanonical URLneolemon.com/guides/...-prompt/You've got a cartoon character idea in your head. But when you type your prompt, the AI gives you... something else entirely. Here's the fix.The Anatomy of a Perfect PromptEvery great character prompt follows the same structure:Who → Features → Outfit → Personality💡The AI cartoon prompt formula looks like this:[Who they are] + [Specific features] + [What they wear] + (Optional) [Personality]Who they are: A young girl, an elderly wizard, a golden retriever puppy.Specific features: Long brown hair and bangs, bright curious eyes, fluffy fur.What they wear: Yellow raincoat and boots, purple robes with golden stars, red collar with a bone-shaped tag.Personality (optional): Shy but kind, wise and caring, curious and adventurous.That last part matters. A short phrase like "curious and adventurous" gives your character depth and shapes how they show up in every scene.But it's a bonus, not a replacement. The visual details always come first.Example in action:❌ "A young girl who loves exploring nature and is always curious about the world around her"This tells us who she is inside, but not what she looks like. The AI has nothing to draw.✅ "An 8-year-old girl with short brown hair and freckles, wearing a yellow raincoat and red boots. She's curious and loves adventure."Now the AI knows what to draw AND who she is. The personality shapes her expressions and body language naturally.5 Prompting Rules That Make All the DifferenceWriting prompts for image generation isn't like chatting with ChatGPT. These five rules will dramatically improve your results.1. Describe one specific momentBreak your story into clear, visual scenes. Don't paste your entire story as a prompt.✅ "Girl reading a book in the library"
❌ "Girl goes to library, finds a book, reads, then leaves"2. Focus on ONE character per promptCreate characters individually first. Combine them in a scene later using Multi-Character tools.✅ "A girl with brown hair and bangs, wearing a yellow raincoat and boots"
❌ "A girl and boy playing together"3. Use positive phrasing onlySay what you want, not what you don't want. Phrases like "no hat" or "without glasses" confuse the AI.✅ "Clean-shaven" instead of "no beard"
✅ "Clear blue sky" instead of "no clouds"4. Be specific, not vagueStay away from words like "maybe," "a bit like," or "kind of."✅ "A man typing fast on a keyboard"
❌ "A man hacking the system"5. Write full prompts every timeDon't assume the AI remembers your last image. Always write the complete description.✅ "Boy with curly hair wearing a yellow raincoat, holding an umbrella"
❌ "Use the last one but make him hold an umbrella"The Anchor Image TrickFor your first generation, always use this action:"Full body front view, standing, smiling"This creates the perfect reference image. Everything after (different poses, expressions, scenes) stays consistent because the AI has a clean foundation to work from.Open Character Turbo. Fill in the three fields:Description: Young boy with curly red hair and big round glasses, wearing a green sweater and brown pants. He's inventive and a little mischievous.Action: Full body front view, standing and smilingBackground: Plain white backgroundHit generate.
